The gap

Larger companies are pulling ahead. Smaller ones aren't.

This isn't a hunch. The U.S. Census Bureau tracks how many businesses use AI, and the split by company size is stark.

Between December 2025 and May 2026, AI use rose among firms with 20 or more employees. Among firms with fewer than 20, it didn't change much at all. The distance between the two is growing while the tools get cheaper.

Closing that gap doesn't take a technology department. It takes knowing which two or three things to do first.

Why most of it doesn't stick

Buying AI is easy. Getting paid back for it isn't.

Small law firms are a useful example, because someone measured them. In Clio's 2026 survey of more than 1,700 legal professionals, 71% of solo practitioners and 75% of small firms reported using AI — but fewer than a third saw revenue increase, against roughly 60% of enterprise firms. Clio calls it the efficiency paradox: working faster hasn't meant earning more.

The reason they give is mundane. Small firms reach for general-purpose consumer tools that need constant re-prompting, miss the context of the work, and raise real confidentiality questions when client information gets pasted into a public chatbot.

That's the difference between using AI and putting it to work. It's the part we handle.

Questions

The things people ask first

If yours isn't here, ask it on the call. Straight answers are the whole point.

Is our data safe?

It depends entirely on which tools you use and how they're configured, which is exactly why it's worth getting right at the start. Consumer chatbots and business tools have very different terms about retention and training. Clio's 2026 research found that small firms leaning on general-purpose consumer tools create real confidentiality risk when client information gets pasted into a public platform.

Part of every engagement is deciding what may go into which tool, and writing that down so your team isn't guessing.

What does this cost?

Each engagement is quoted on its own. After the free discovery call you get a written scope and a fixed number before anything starts. No hourly meter, no surprises at the end.

We're small and nobody here is technical. Is this for us?

That's who this is for. Businesses with technology departments already have people doing this work. According to the U.S. Census Bureau, under 20% of firms with four or fewer employees use AI, against 37% of firms with 250 or more.

You don't need anyone technical on staff. That's the point of hiring someone for it.

Will this replace our staff?

That isn't what the evidence shows. The Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia surveyed firms across Delaware, southern New Jersey, and eastern and central Pennsylvania in late October 2025. Among those using generative AI, about 70% said it hadn't changed their need for workers and 17% said it changed the kind of workers they needed rather than the number. Eight percent reported a decrease. It was a small sample — 48 firms using the technology — so treat it as a signal, not a law.

Our work targets the repetitive tasks nobody wanted in the first place: retyping, chasing, filing, formatting.

What does a first engagement look like?

A free 30-minute discovery call, then usually an AI and workflow opportunity assessment. We spend time understanding how work moves through your business, then give you a written roadmap ranked by payback. You keep it either way, and you decide whether we build anything.

How long does it take?

The assessment is typically a couple of weeks, depending on how much of your team we need time with. A first automation is usually live within a few weeks after that. We deliberately start with one thing rather than building everything at once.

What if AI isn't the right answer for us?

Then we say so, and you've spent thirty minutes finding out. Sometimes the honest recommendation is a better process, a setting already in software you own, or simply waiting. We don't resell tools and we don't take vendor commissions, so there's nothing pushing us to tell you otherwise.

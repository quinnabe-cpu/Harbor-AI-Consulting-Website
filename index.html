AI use is rising at larger companies. At smaller ones it isn't.

This isn't a hunch. The U.S. Census Bureau tracks how many businesses use AI. Among firms with 250 or more employees, 37% use it. Among firms with four or fewer, under 20% do.

Between December 2025 and May 2026, AI use rose among firms with 20 or more employees. Among firms with fewer than 20, it didn't change significantly.

Construction is behind the national rate. As of May 3, 2026, 12.3% of construction businesses used AI, against 19.8% of all businesses.

Closing that gap doesn't take a technology department. It takes knowing which two or three things to do first.

Buying AI is easy. Getting paid back for it isn't.

Small law firms are a useful example, because someone measured them. In Clio's 2026 survey of more than 1,700 respondents, 71% of solo practitioners and 75% of small firms reported using AI, but fewer than a third had increased revenue with it, against nearly 60% of enterprise firms. Clio calls it the efficiency paradox: working faster hasn't meant earning more.

The reason Clio gives is ordinary. Most small firms use general-purpose consumer tools. Those tools need constant re-prompting, lack the context of the work, and create a confidentiality risk when client information is entered into a public chatbot.

Using AI and earning more from it are two different things. The second is the part we handle.

Source: Clio, 2026 Legal Trends Report for Solo and Small Law Firms, May 2026.

Four kinds of work worth handing over

These four kinds of work are repetitive in most offices. We start here because the tasks repeat and the results are easy to check.

Customer communication

Answering routine questions, drafting replies, following up on quotes, responding to reviews, and covering the hours nobody's at a desk.

Documents and paperwork

Pulling data out of invoices, contracts, and intake forms. Summarizing long documents. Turning notes into a quote or a proposal.

Marketing and content

Social posts, email campaigns, service pages, and product descriptions written in your voice instead of a generic one.

Internal operations

A searchable assistant over your own procedures and files, plus scheduling, data entry, reporting, and keeping the customer records accurate.

The things people ask first

If yours isn't here, ask it on the call. We answer plainly.

Is our data safe?

It depends entirely on which tools you use and how they're configured, which is exactly why it's worth getting right at the start. Consumer chatbots and business tools have very different terms about retention and training. Clio's 2026 research found that small firms relying on general-purpose consumer tools create a real confidentiality risk when client information is entered into a public platform.

Part of every engagement is deciding what may go into which tool, and writing that down so your team isn't guessing.

What does this cost?

Each engagement is quoted on its own. After the free discovery call you get a written scope and a fixed number before anything starts. No hourly billing. The number doesn't change after you agree to it.

We're small and nobody here is technical. Is this for us?

That's who this is for. Businesses with technology departments already have people doing this work. According to the U.S. Census Bureau, under 20% of firms with four or fewer employees use AI, against 37% of firms with 250 or more.

You don't need anyone technical on staff. That's the point of hiring someone for it.

Will this replace our staff?

That isn't what the evidence shows. The Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia surveyed firms across Delaware, southern New Jersey, and eastern and central Pennsylvania in late October 2025. Among those using generative AI, almost 70% said it hadn't changed their need for workers and 17% said it changed the kind of workers they needed rather than the number. Eight percent reported a decrease. It was a small sample (48 firms using the technology), so it is an early indication, not proof.

Our work targets the repetitive tasks nobody wanted in the first place: retyping, following up, filing, formatting.

What does a first engagement look like?

A free 30-minute discovery call, then an assessment of your workflows and where AI fits. We spend time understanding how work moves through your business, then give you a written roadmap ranked by payback. You keep it either way, and you decide whether we build anything.

How long does it take?

We plan about two weeks for the assessment, depending on how much of your team we need time with. We plan for the first automation to be live a few weeks after that. We deliberately start with one thing rather than building everything at once.

What if AI isn't the right answer for us?

Then we say so, and you've spent 30 minutes finding out. The honest recommendation can be a better process, a setting already in software you own, or waiting. We don't resell tools and we don't take vendor commissions, so we have no reason to tell you otherwise.
